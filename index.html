The People

The AI Doc: Or How I Became an Apocaloptimist was made by Daniel Roher and Charlie Tyrell. It looks at people's thoughts and feelings about AI in the near future. It asks if we should be scared or excited about AI.

02 / Interviews

People in
The AI Doc

These five people are interviewed in the documentary.

Google DeepMind

Shane Legg

Works at: Google DeepMind · Role: Co-founder and Chief AGI Scientist

Legg earned his PhD at IDSIA. He helped build DeepMind and its work on artificial general intelligence. DeepMind worked on deep reinforcement learning. This lets AI learn from experience, such as playing Atari games. Legg leads the technical AGI safety team. He has spoken about AI risk and signed statements about AI safety. He received the CBE and the Digital Masters Award. Source ↗

“Eventually, I think human extinction will probably occur, and technology will likely play a part in this. But there's a big difference between this being within a year of something like human level AI, and within a million years. As for the former meaning...I don't know. Maybe 5%, maybe 50%. I don't think anybody has a good estimate of this.”

— Shane Legg, source ↗

Machine Intelligence Research Institute

Eliezer Yudkowsky

Works at: Machine Intelligence Research Institute · Role: Co-founder and longtime AI-risk theorist

Yudkowsky helped start the Machine Intelligence Research Institute and the field of AI alignment. He started LessWrong in 2009 and has written hundreds of articles about AI risks. He wrote chapters for The Cambridge Handbook of Artificial Intelligence and Global Catastrophic Risks. He gave a TED Talk and proposed ways to limit AI. One proposal was to let the government destroy a rogue data center by airstrike. The slides say he is very worried about AI. Source ↗

UC Berkeley

Deborah Raji

Works at: UC Berkeley · Role: Researcher in AI auditing and accountability

Raji works on AI bias and its effects on people. She is a member of the Mozilla Foundation. She also worked with the Algorithmic Justice League on facial recognition and its effects on marginalized communities. She is on advisory boards for AI governance, health AI, and civil rights. She has been named to Forbes 30 Under 30, MIT Technology Review's 35 Under 35, and TIME 100 in AI. The slides say she is worried about AI's dangers. Source ↗

“Let’s say I want examples of what healthy skin looks like. If you Google it now, you will see mostly light-skinned women,” says Raji. “You won’t see a man for pages, and you wouldn’t see a darker-skinned woman until you really scroll down. If you feed that into an AI model, it adopts this world view and adapts its decisions based on those biases.”

— Deborah Raji, source ↗

The Washington Post

Nitasha Tiku

Works at: The Washington Post · Role: Technology culture reporter

Tiku studied English at Columbia University and journalism at New York University. She has written for WIRED, BuzzFeed News, The Verge, Valleywag, and New York Magazine. At The Washington Post, she reports on technology culture. She writes about how AI affects people and society, including AI companions and relationships. The slides say she is skeptical and concerned about AI. Source ↗

XPRIZE and Singularity University

Peter Diamandis

Works at: XPRIZE and Singularity University · Role: Founder and chair

Diamandis founded XPRIZE. Its contests have awarded over $600 million and supported more than $10 billion in research and development. He co-founded Singularity University, Ling Exponential Adventures, and BOLD Adventures. He has degrees in molecular genetics and aerospace engineering, and an MD from Harvard Medical School. He is a bestselling author. The slides say he has a positive view of AI. Sources ↗
